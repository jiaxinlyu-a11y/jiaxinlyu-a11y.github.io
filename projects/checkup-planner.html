Check-up Planner

I built the Check-up Planner to answer one question: which check-ups apply to me, and how sure are we? You enter age, sex, habits, work environment and family history. It lists the matching items, each with an evidence level and its sources, in English or Chinese.

01Why I built it

A standard hospital check-up package is much the same for everyone. The guidelines say something different: who should be screened for what depends on age, sex, smoking, drinking, work and family history. Those recommendations are spread across separate guidelines from different countries, and the numbers do not always agree. Cervical screening starts at 21 in the US guideline and at 25 in the Chinese one. I wanted one place that matches a person to the items that apply and shows where each one comes from.

02How it works

I wrote the rules first, as a table. Each rule is one check-up item, who it applies to, an evidence level and its sources. The page compares your answers with the table and shows the rows that match, grouped by age and sex, lifestyle, risk group and work environment, and family history. A row shows a one-line reason; opening it shows the details and the sources.

Most items come from the US Preventive Services Task Force, with guidance from the American Cancer Society and the American College of Gastroenterology. Where Chinese guidelines exist, such as the 2023 lipid and cervical screening guidelines and the 2018 low-dose CT lung screening guideline, the item gives both sets of numbers instead of two separate lists. The workplace items come from OSHA and from the clauses of the Chinese standard GBZ 188-2025 for noise, silica dust, lead, benzene, formaldehyde, toluene and xylene, and heat.

03How it handles weak evidence

Not every useful rule has a guideline behind it. Habit-based triggers, such as frequent takeout for an H. pylori test or short sleep for blood pressure and glucose checks, are labelled Consider, and the item says in plain words that it is an inference or an association. Each rule carries one of five levels, and the level sets the number of stars next to it.

The last level works the other way. Some items are common in check-up packages, but a guideline advises against them for people without symptoms or a known risk, because false alarms and overdiagnosis can do more harm than good. There are nine, among them thyroid ultrasound, tumor-marker blood tests and whole-body CT. The planner lists them in their own group at the end, with no stars, and each row says in a sentence or two who advises against it and why, since the reason is less obvious than for a recommended screening. Where the source is a review or an old statement rather than a guideline, the row says so.

Two rules keep the tool honest. If I could not check a number against the original text, I left it out; for example, Chinese figures that I could only find second-hand were removed or marked as second-hand. And the tool recommends no supplements, only tests.

It is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no framework. Everything runs in the browser, so nothing a user enters is sent anywhere or stored, and the English and Chinese versions use the same rules.
