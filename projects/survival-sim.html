Case study · Simulation study

Which survival test finds the effect?

I designed, coded and ran this simulation study myself, in R. It compares three common ways to test a treatment effect in survival data, under four kinds of true effect, and reports false-alarm rate, power and bias for each. The whole project is reproducible from a public repository.

01The question and the design

The Cox hazard ratio, the log-rank test and the restricted mean survival time (RMST) difference answer slightly different questions. I wanted to see how often each one finds a real difference, and where they disagree. I planned the study with the ADEMP framework: aims, data-generating mechanism, estimand, methods and performance measures.

02Four kinds of true effect

Data come from known survival curves, so the truth is never in doubt. One scenario has no effect at all and measures false alarms. The other three have an effect that is constant, delayed, or reverses direction at month 6.

03When each method finds the effect

With a constant effect, Cox and log-rank find it more often than RMST at every sample size. With the delayed effect the gap is larger: at 300 per group and 20% censoring, Cox rejects in 0.997 of runs and RMST in 0.727. When the curves cross, Cox’s power climbs from 0.331 to 0.75 to 0.965 as the sample grows, while RMST stays near 0.05.

04Can the numbers be trusted?

Power only means something if the tests do not fire when there is nothing to find. Under the null, all three tests reject between 0.043 and 0.066 of the time, close to the 0.05 target; each cell carries a Monte Carlo standard error of about 0.007. The RMST test sits at 0.066 in the three 50%-censored cells, slightly high, which I note rather than hide.

05What it means, and the limits

No single test wins everywhere. Cox and log-rank are more powerful when the effect is constant or delayed, but when the curves cross they flag a difference that the 24-month average hides. The practical point is to choose the estimand first, then the test that answers it.

The limits are real: one baseline hazard (0.05 per month), a single switch at month 6, two groups, independent censoring, one RMST horizon of 24 months, and 1,000 repetitions, so a rate in the middle of the range carries a Monte Carlo error of up to about 0.016.
